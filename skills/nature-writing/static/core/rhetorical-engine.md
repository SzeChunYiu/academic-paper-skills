# Rhetorical engine

Academic writing is not a fixed template. It is a sequence of reader-facing rhetorical moves whose preferred order and linguistic realization depend on discipline, paper type, evidence, and venue.

Use this engine before sentence-level drafting.

## 1. Build the argument spine

Represent the paper with five linked objects:

1. **Question / tension** — what is not yet settled, explained, measured, compared, demonstrated, synthesized, or made possible?
2. **Answer / contribution** — what does this paper establish, provide, change, or clarify?
3. **Evidence chain** — which observations, experiments, analyses, proofs, cases, comparisons, or sources make that answer credible?
4. **Boundary** — under what conditions does the answer hold, and where does it stop?
5. **Meaning** — why does the bounded answer matter to the intended research community?

A paper can have several contributions. Keep one dominant spine and attach secondary contribution branches instead of forcing everything into one sentence.

## 2. Separate contribution type from novelty language

Classify each contribution before framing it:

- empirical finding / discovery
- mechanism / explanation
- method / algorithm / instrument
- dataset / resource / benchmark
- theory / proof / model
- replication / validation / robustness result
- negative or null result
- synthesis / review / taxonomy
- clinical / practical / policy implication

Do not make an incremental contribution sound non-incremental by hiding its relationship to prior work. Position it accurately: what changes, how much, under which conditions, and why that difference matters.

## 3. Plan sections as move graphs, not templates

A **move** is a communicative function such as establishing context, identifying a tension, stating a result, interpreting evidence, comparing with prior work, or delimiting a claim.

- Moves may repeat.
- A move may span several paragraphs.
- A paragraph may contain one main move plus supporting submoves.
- Move order may change by field and paper type.
- A journal skeleton never overrides the logic of the evidence.

Use section fragments for likely move inventories, then adapt them to the material. For deeper cross-disciplinary options, load `references/section-move-atlas.md`.

## 4. Give every paragraph a nucleus, not an artificial single job

Each paragraph needs one **nucleus**: the proposition or reader task that makes the paragraph necessary.

It may also contain **satellites** that serve the nucleus:

- evidence
- explanation / mechanism
- example
- comparison
- qualification / caveat
- counterargument
- implication
- bridge to the next move

Split a paragraph when it contains two independent nuclei that could survive separately. Do not split merely because two rhetorical functions appear in the same paragraph.

## 5. Build sentence-to-sentence information flow

For each sentence, identify:

- **given** — information already active for the reader
- **new** — the information this sentence adds
- **relation** — cause, contrast, consequence, specification, example, evidence, concession, sequence, or inference

Prefer a readable given-to-new progression when it fits. Keep important recurring entities lexically stable enough that the reader can track them. Do not insert transition words merely to create the appearance of flow; make the logical relation real.

Place the sentence's main emphasis where readers can find it easily. If a sentence contains several clauses, make sure only one is carrying the primary new claim.

## 6. Match syntax to rhetorical function

Do not optimize academic prose for uniformly short sentences.

- Dense technical definitions and established relations may need compact noun phrases.
- Causal reasoning, qualification, and theoretical argument may need explicit clauses.
- Procedures often benefit from chronological syntax.
- Results benefit from a clear separation between observation, comparison, and interpretation.

Complexity is useful when it compresses a relation the reader already understands; it is harmful when it hides the relation the reader is trying to discover.

## 7. Calibrate epistemic stance locally

Choose claim strength from the evidence and the rhetorical move, not from prestige style.

Ask:

- Is this directly observed, estimated, inferred, simulated, proved, or hypothesized?
- Is the statement descriptive, causal, mechanistic, predictive, or generalizing?
- What uncertainty, model dependence, sample dependence, or boundary accompanies it?

Use strong verbs only when the evidence warrants them. A Discussion interpretation can be more tentative than the corresponding Results observation without being weaker writing.

## 8. Preserve section-to-section continuity

Each section should inherit a live question from the previous one and leave the reader with the next useful question.

Typical handoffs include:

- Introduction: **Why this question, and what will answer it?**
- Methods: **Can I trust how the answer was obtained?**
- Results: **What did the evidence actually establish?**
- Discussion: **What does that evidence mean, relative to alternatives and prior knowledge?**
- Conclusion: **What remains true after all qualifications are included?**

These are reader questions, not mandatory headings.

## 9. Run the reader-prediction test

After every paragraph, ask:

1. What does a competent skeptical reader now believe?
2. What question is that reader most likely to ask next?
3. Does the next paragraph answer or deliberately redirect that question?
4. If not, is there a missing bridge, missing evidence, or wrong paragraph order?

Use this test to diagnose logic before polishing wording.

## 10. Apply journal style last

First make the argument scientifically coherent and field-appropriate. Then apply the exact target journal's section structure, length, title/abstract conventions, reference rendering, and submission mechanics.

Journal adaptation may change packaging and emphasis. It must not change the evidence, causal strength, uncertainty, novelty boundary, or limitations.
